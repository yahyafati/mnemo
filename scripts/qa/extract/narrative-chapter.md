# Chapter 3: The Weight of a Loss

Tomas Varga had fished the same stretch of the Danube for thirty-one years. Every morning at half past four he
pushed his flat-bottomed boat into the current, and every evening he sold what he caught to the restaurant on the
corner of Fő utca. In the spring of 1998 a buyer from a cannery offered him a contract: a fixed price for his whole
catch, every week, for five years. On average the contract paid more than the restaurant did. Tomas turned it down.
When his daughter asked him why, he said only that he could not bear the thought of the weeks when the cannery would
pay less than the restaurant would have.

His daughter, who was studying economics in Budapest at the time, told me this story twenty years later, and she
still laughed when she told it. Her father had done the arithmetic. He knew the contract was the better deal. What
he could not do was stop counting the bad weeks.

## Losses loom larger than gains

The pattern Tomas fell into has a name. **Loss aversion** is the tendency to feel a loss more strongly than a gain
of the same size. Losing a hundred euros hurts more than finding a hundred euros pleases. In experiments where people
are offered a coin toss, most of them refuse a bet that would win them 100 and lose them 100 with equal chance, and
many still refuse when the possible win is raised to 150. On average people want the possible gain to be roughly
**twice** the possible loss before they accept such a bet. That ratio, around two to one, is called the
**loss-aversion coefficient**.

Loss aversion is one half of **prospect theory**, the account of decisions under risk that Daniel Kahneman and Amos
Tversky published in 1979. Its other central claim is that people judge outcomes as gains or losses **relative to a
reference point**, usually what they have now or what they expected, rather than by the final amount of wealth they
end up with.

I remember the first time I saw this in a classroom. A colleague of mine, who taught a night course for adults,
handed out mugs to half of the room and asked the other half what they would pay for one. Then she asked the owners
what they would sell theirs for. The owners asked for more than twice what the buyers offered. One of the buyers, a
retired bus driver, grew so annoyed at the prices that he declared mugs to be a racket and went home early. The
mugs had cost her a little over three euros each.

## The endowment effect

What my colleague's class showed is the **endowment effect**: people value a thing more once they own it. Giving up
the mug is coded as a loss, buying one as a gain, and because losses weigh more, the selling price ends up well above
the buying price. The effect is one of the clearest consequences of loss aversion, and it appears even when people
have owned the object for only a few minutes.

The endowment effect is weaker for things held only to be exchanged. Traders do not feel the loss of the shares they
sell in the way a mug owner feels the loss of the mug, and money spent on ordinary purchases is not experienced as a
loss at all.

## Framing

Because the reference point decides what counts as a loss, the same choice can feel different when it is described
differently. This is the **framing effect**. A treatment described as having a "90 percent survival rate" is chosen
far more often than the same treatment described as having a "10 percent mortality rate", even by doctors.

My grandmother understood framing before anyone named it. She ran a small grocery in a village near Szeged, and she
never raised a price. Instead she gave a "discount for paying in cash" that was a little smaller every year.
Customers grumbled about many things, but never about her prices.

## Back on the river

Tomas, for what it is worth, never regretted his decision. The restaurant on Fő utca closed in 2004, and he spent his
last working years selling to the cannery after all, at a lower price than the one he had been offered. He told his
daughter it was still better than the bad weeks would have been.

Loss aversion is not a mistake in every case. A farmer who cannot survive one terrible season is right to weigh the
terrible season heavily. The trouble starts when the same instinct is applied to many small, repeated choices, where
the good and bad weeks would even out, and where refusing every bet with a possible loss means refusing almost every
bet worth taking.
